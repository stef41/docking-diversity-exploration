\title{Diversity-Seeking Docking-Guided Molecular Design Discovers Broader Predicted Interaction Profiles Across Protein Families}

\abbreviations{IMGEP, QD, MAP-Elites, PLIP, CReM, GNINA, NSGA-II, IFP}

\keywords{molecular docking, de novo molecular design, interaction fingerprints, quality-diversity, intrinsically motivated goal exploration, hit diversification}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Structure-based discovery pipelines commonly prioritize a scalar docking score, concentrating candidates into narrow chemotypes and contact patterns. We formulate docking-guided \emph{de novo} molecular design as a diversity-seeking exploration problem and compare goal-directed, quality-diversity, Pareto-optimization, affinity-focused, novelty-based, and random search policies. Molecules are generated by fragment-based CReM edits and scored by rigid-receptor GNINA/Vina docking. We introduce a residue-level multi-interaction fingerprint encoding hydrophobic contacts and hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor interactions, together with the \emph{reachability ratio}, an empirical diagnostic of goal-retrieval discriminability. Across seven targets from five protein families, with 500 docking evaluations per run, MAP-Elites discovers $13.6\%$ more interaction profiles than random search ($d = 1.65$, $p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} < 10^{-13}$), while Curiosity-IMGEP produces $+21\%$ more Bemis--Murcko scaffolds and $+16\%$ more binary residue-contact patterns. A newly added budget-matched NSGA-II baseline matches the Genetic Algorithm on best predicted affinity ($-11.18$ vs.\ $-11.22$~kcal/mol; $d = -1.08$ vs.\ Random, $p_\mathrm{Bonf} < 10^{-8}$) and leads on Bemis--Murcko scaffold count ($24.6 \pm 12.3$), but underperforms QD/goal-directed methods on interaction-profile counts ($282 \pm 30$, $-8\%$ vs.\ Random). Representation ablations compare continuous and binary fingerprints, charged-interaction channels, distance cutoffs, and inclusion of docking quality in the search vector. Target-specific ChEMBL neighborhood and pose-robustness analyses provide orthogonal computational triage. The results establish that diversity-aware search broadens the set of docking-predicted interaction hypotheses generated under a fixed budget; they do not establish experimentally distinct binding modes or biochemical activity. Code, configurations, and raw results are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Conventional docking-based virtual screening ranks compounds from a predefined collection, usually using a scalar estimate of binding quality.\cite{kitchen2004docking,lyu2019ultra} By contrast, the workflow studied here is a docking-guided \emph{de novo} molecular-design process: new structures are generated iteratively through fragment growth or replacement and are selected using the outcomes of previous docking evaluations. Both settings commonly prioritize affinity-like scores.\cite{gentile2020deep} This can concentrate computational effort on a narrow set of chemotypes and predicted contacts, even when the pocket admits several plausible residue-engagement hypotheses.\cite{huggins2012binding}

Discovering this interaction diversity has direct practical value. A compound predicted to engage a catalytic lysine via a hydrogen bond represents a different selectivity hypothesis than one relying on hydrophobic packing in the same sub-pocket; mutations at residues contacted by only one predicted binding mode would spare alternatives.\cite{diversity_drug_design} Diversity-oriented synthesis maximizes chemical structural variety,\cite{schreiber2000target} but chemical diversity is a poor proxy for interaction diversity: structurally similar compounds can exhibit markedly different interaction profiles, and vice versa.\cite{stumpfe2012activity}

Protein--ligand interaction fingerprints provide a compact representation of residue-level contacts and have been used for pose analysis, post-docking filtering, and scaffold hopping.\cite{deng2004sift,marcou2007mifp,da2014splif,venhorst2008scaffold,jasper2018padif} Most prior applications rank candidates by similarity to a reference interaction pattern. Our question is different: when no single reference pattern is assumed, can a search policy deliberately broaden the set of predicted interaction patterns obtained under a fixed evaluation budget?

The motivation is not that every distinct docking fingerprint corresponds to a stable experimental binding mode. Rather, interaction-diverse generation is a hypothesis-portfolio strategy. Candidates that rely on different predicted residues or interaction types may respond differently to downstream rescoring, protein conformations, mutations, and medicinal-chemistry constraints. The portfolio is useful only if followed by orthogonal computational or experimental triage; interaction diversity is therefore evaluated here as an upstream search objective, not as evidence of binding by itself.

We pursue this objective algorithmically, using two families of diversity-seeking search methods from the machine learning literature. \textbf{Quality-diversity (QD) algorithms}, particularly MAP-Elites,\cite{mouret2015illuminating,pugh2016qd} maintain a behavioral archive mapping discrete cells to their best-performing solutions, providing explicit coverage pressure. \textbf{Intrinsically motivated goal exploration processes (IMGEP)},\cite{baranes2013active,forestier2017intrinsically} developed for robotic skill discovery, set self-generated goals in a behavior space, retrieve the nearest known solution, perturb it, and evaluate the result; curiosity-driven variants bias goal selection toward under-explored regions.\cite{colas2022autotelic,etcheverry2020hierarchically}

Both families are designed to spread coverage across the space of possible outcomes rather than converge to optima, precisely the desideratum for interaction-profile discovery. Quality-diversity and intrinsically motivated goal-exploration methods have not, to our knowledge, been systematically compared for maximizing the diversity of docking-predicted residue-interaction profiles during iterative molecular generation. This differs from recent interaction-fingerprint-guided generation that optimizes toward desired or reference interactions rather than illuminating the range of reachable interaction outcomes.\cite{wang2026ifpgen}

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A residue-level interaction representation and the \emph{reachability ratio}, an empirical diagnostic of goal-retrieval discriminability.
    \item A controlled multi-target benchmark of diversity-aware, multiobjective, affinity-focused, novelty-based, and random search policies under a common CReM and docking budget (seven proteins from five structural families).
    \item Representation ablations covering binary encoding, charged interactions, distance scaling, and separation of docking quality from the interaction descriptor.
    \item Evidence, evaluated at multiple levels of independence, that diversity-aware search broadens the chemical structures and docking-predicted interaction hypotheses submitted to downstream triage.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Protein target benchmark}

We selected seven therapeutically relevant proteins from five structural families (Table~\ref{tab:targets}). Crystal structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank.\cite{berman2000protein} Chain~A was extracted, water molecules and ions removed, and the binding box defined from co-crystallized ligand coordinates with 10~\AA\ padding (capped at 27~\AA). Figure~\ref{fig:docking3d} illustrates the kind of binding-mode diversity our methods seek to discover within a single pocket.

For each target, the \emph{extended pocket residue set} was defined by inclusion of every residue with at least one atom whose Cartesian coordinates fall inside the axis-aligned docking box, that is, satisfying $|x - c_x| \leq s_x/2$, $|y - c_y| \leq s_y/2$, and $|z - c_z| \leq s_z/2$, where $(c_x, c_y, c_z)$ and $(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ are the box center and size. The set was fixed before search and was shared by all methods on a given target. Because a box-derived set can include residues on the periphery of the accessible pocket, we additionally define a \emph{co-crystal-local pocket set} consisting of residues with at least one protein heavy atom within $4.0$~\AA\ of any co-crystallized-ligand heavy atom (matching the maximum interaction distance $d_{\max}$ used in Section~\nameref{sec:fingerprint}). Coverage is reported against both denominators.

\subsection{Interaction representation and docking quality}
\label{sec:fingerprint}

For a generated molecule $m$, let $\boldsymbol{\psi}(m)\in[0,1]^{3N_{\mathrm{res}}}$ denote its interaction-only fingerprint, where $N_{\mathrm{res}}$ is the number of residues in the target-specific extended pocket set. For residue $r\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{res}}\}$ and interaction type $t\in\{\mathrm{hydrophobic},\mathrm{HBD},\mathrm{HBA}\}$, we define the interaction fingerprint entry-wise per Equation~\ref{eq:fingerprint}:
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{r,t}(m) = \text{clip}\left(\frac{d_\text{max} - d_\text{nearest}(m, r, t)}{d_\text{max} - d_\text{min}}, \; 0, \; 1\right),
\label{eq:fingerprint}
\end{equation}
where $d_\text{nearest}(m, r, t)$ is the shortest distance associated with an interaction of type $t$ between molecule $m$ and residue $r$ as reported by PLIP.\cite{salentin2015plip} If PLIP reports no interaction of type $t$ with residue $r$, we set $\psi_{r,t}(m) = 0$. We use $d_\text{min} = 1.5$~\AA\ and $d_\text{max} = 4.0$~\AA, following standard PLIP non-covalent interaction cutoffs.\cite{salentin2015plip} Contacts at or below $d_\text{min}$ saturate at $1$; values between the thresholds decrease linearly; values at or above $d_\text{max}$ are $0$. Sensitivity to these thresholds is quantified in Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}; Supporting Information Section~S11d reports the corresponding cutoff-sensitivity data.

The docking quality is represented separately by Equation~\ref{eq:quality}:
\begin{equation}
    q(m) = \text{clip}\left(-s_\text{Vina}(m)/12,\; 0,\; 1\right),
\label{eq:quality}
\end{equation}
where $s_\text{Vina}(m)$ is the Vina score in kcal/mol. The submitted implementation appended this scalar to the interaction descriptor for selected parent-selection procedures, forming an augmented vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}(m) = [\boldsymbol{\psi}(m), q(m)] \in [0,1]^{3N_{\mathrm{res}}+1}$. MAP-Elites cell assignment did \emph{not} use $q(m)$: cells were defined from binary residue-contact patterns and $q(m)$ was used only as the archive quality criterion. In the revised analysis, all metrics termed ``interaction-profile counts'' or ``behavioral distances'' are computed from $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ alone.

The three interaction types capture the dominant non-covalent forces in protein--ligand recognition. Finer categories (e.g., $\pi$-stacking, halogen bonds) may increase descriptor sparsity, whose consequences for goal-directed retrieval are diagnosed via the reachability ratio (Section~\nameref{sec:reachability}). Residue-level aggregation (vs.\ atom-level) was motivated by the reachability analysis: it increases activation density from $<1\%$ to $\sim$2--3\% of dimensions.

\paragraph{Binary baseline.} A conventional binary interaction fingerprint is defined by Equation~\ref{eq:binary}:
\begin{equation}
    \psi^{\mathrm{bin}}_{r,t}(m) = \mathbb{I}\left[\psi_{r,t}(m) > 0\right].
\label{eq:binary}
\end{equation}
The continuous representation has the same nonzero support as the corresponding binary representation. Its intended role is not to increase density, but to preserve graded distance information and reduce nearest-neighbor ties during goal retrieval. Binary residue-level interaction fingerprints are well established (SIFt, MIFP, SPLIF)\cite{deng2004sift,marcou2007mifp,da2014splif}; continuous and score-weighted variants also have precedent, e.g., PADIF, which represents interactions using per-atom score contributions rather than presence/absence alone.\cite{jasper2018padif} Section~\nameref{sec:ablations} compares the two encodings directly on all seven targets.

\paragraph{Charged-channel ablation.} In a targeted ablation, two additional directional channels encode salt bridges: ligand-positive/protein-negative and ligand-negative/protein-positive. These channels are evaluated separately because their prevalence and effect on reachability are strongly pocket dependent.

\subsection{Reachability ratio: a behavior-space diagnostic}
\label{sec:reachability}

Whether goal-directed exploration outperforms random search depends on whether nearest-neighbor retrieval is \emph{discriminative}: different sampled goals should select different parents. Let $\mathcal{B} = \{\boldsymbol{\psi}(m_1), \ldots, \boldsymbol{\psi}(m_n)\}$ be the interaction behaviors observed after $n$ evaluations, and let $P_\mathrm{goal}$ denote the goal distribution used by an IMGEP variant. We define three quantities via Equations~\ref{eq:dgoal}--\ref{eq:reach}:
\begin{align}
    D_\text{goal} &= \mathbb{E}_{g \sim P_\text{goal}}\left[\min_{\boldsymbol{b} \in \mathcal{B}} \|g - \boldsymbol{b}\|_2\right],  \label{eq:dgoal} \\
    D_\text{inter} &= \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{b} \neq \boldsymbol{b}' \sim \mathcal{B}}\left[\|\boldsymbol{b} - \boldsymbol{b}'\|_2\right], \label{eq:dinter} \\
    R &= D_\text{goal} / D_\text{inter}. \label{eq:reach}
\end{align}
Equation~\ref{eq:dgoal} measures how far sampled goals lie from the observed behavior set; Equation~\ref{eq:dinter} measures the separation among observed behaviors; and the reachability ratio in Equation~\ref{eq:reach} indicates that when $R$ is large, differences among candidate parents are small relative to the goal distance and retrieval degenerates toward random parent selection.

Our residue-level fingerprint reduces $R$ from $\sim$128 (atom-level baseline, computed on the augmented $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ used in the submitted implementation) to $\sim$8 (residue-level $\boldsymbol{\phi}$), primarily by increasing $D_\text{inter}$ through residue-level aggregation and multi-interaction encoding. Adaptive goal sampling (perturbing observed behaviors rather than sampling uniformly) further reduces $R$ to $\sim$0.3. The corresponding values for the interaction-only $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ and for the binary $\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\mathrm{bin}}$ are reported in Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}. We evaluate this interpretation using retrieval tie rates, parent-selection concentration, and continuous-versus-binary ablations (Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}). The numerical values discussed below are empirical observations from this benchmark rather than universal thresholds; controlled sweeps across representation dimension, sparsity, and goal distributions remain an important direction.

\subsection{Fragment vocabulary and mutation operator}
\label{sec:crem}

All methods used CReM v0.2.16\cite{polishchuk2020crem} as a shared molecular-editing engine. The production runs used the replacement database \texttt{crem\_large.db} (SHA-256: \texttt{40262010822204fdb7576ba842\allowbreak 08512e6203992cfb1fcbe1\allowbreak 57648fde6e6ad664}, $1{,}388{,}544$ bytes), containing $5{,}955$ replacement entries. This is a restricted, synthetic-accessibility-filtered vocabulary derived from the standard CReM sa2 replacement set and therefore defines the chemical space reachable in this benchmark; it is not intended to approximate an exhaustive fragment collection.

For each parent molecule, hydrogen atoms were added and both \texttt{mutate\_mol} and \texttt{grow\_mol} were called with the CReM v0.2.16 library defaults. For \texttt{mutate\_mol}: \texttt{radius}=3, \texttt{min\_size}=0, \texttt{max\_size}=10, \texttt{min\_rel\_size}=0, \texttt{max\_rel\_size}=1, \texttt{min\_inc}=-2, \texttt{max\_inc}=2, \texttt{min\_freq}=0. For \texttt{grow\_mol}: \texttt{radius}=3, \texttt{min\_atoms}=1, \texttt{max\_atoms}=2, \texttt{min\_freq}=0. Candidate enumeration stopped after at most 15 filter-passing candidates had been accumulated. The applied candidate was then sampled uniformly from this set.

Candidates were rejected if RDKit could not parse the SMILES, if molecular weight exceeded $500$~Da, if the number of hydrogen-bond donors exceeded $5$, if the number of hydrogen-bond acceptors exceeded $10$, if the calculated $\log P \geq 5$, or if a single RDKit 3D-embedding attempt (\texttt{AllChem.EmbedMolecule}, \texttt{randomSeed=42}, on the hydrogen-added molecule) failed. If no valid candidate was produced, the unchanged parent SMILES was returned and the iteration still counted toward the 500-evaluation budget. Docking or PLIP failures yielded a zero interaction vector and zero normalized quality, after which parent selection proceeded as documented per method. Supporting Information Section~S5 (\emph{Failure statistics}) reports the observed no-op mutation rate (a proxy for CReM producing no filter-passing candidate), zero-interaction rate, and runtime-error rate by method.

The first five evaluations used the five shared seed molecules directly, without mutation. The seeds were benzylpenicillin, ibuprofen, aspirin, acetaminophen, and salicylic acid. The same seeds were used for every target and were not selected as known ligands of the benchmark proteins. This design isolates differences in search policy but is not intended to represent a target-specific lead-optimization campaign.

\subsection{Exploration algorithms}

All methods share the CReM molecular-editing engine and filters described in Section~\nameref{sec:crem}, together with the GNINA/Vina docking pipeline (Section~\nameref{sec:protocol}). Each operates for $T = 500$ iterations with identical evaluation budgets, and all methods are initialized from the same shared pool of five drug-like seed molecules regardless of the target. Unless stated otherwise, the ``behavior'' vector used by parent-selection procedures below is the augmented vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}(m) = [\boldsymbol{\psi}(m), q(m)] \in [0,1]^{3N_{\mathrm{res}}+1}$ of the submitted implementation (Section~\nameref{sec:fingerprint}); the corresponding search-time ablation that operates on the interaction-only $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ is described in Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}. We compare nine methods:

\textbf{Random.} Uniform parent selection over the history of previously evaluated molecules; a single CReM edit is then applied.

\textbf{IMGEP (naive).} Goals sampled uniformly in $[0,1]^d$; the molecule minimizing Euclidean distance to the goal is selected as parent.

\textbf{IMGEP (adaptive).} Goals sampled as Gaussian perturbations of a uniformly random previously observed behavior: $g = \text{clip}(\boldsymbol{b}_i + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, 0, 1)$ with $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3 \cdot \text{range}(\mathcal{B}))$; parent is the nearest previously observed behavior.

\textbf{Curiosity-IMGEP.} Same as adaptive, but the base behavior $\boldsymbol{b}_i$ is selected with probability proportional to local sparsity (mean Euclidean distance to its $k=5$ nearest neighbors in behavior space), biasing exploration toward under-sampled regions.

\textbf{Affinity--Diversity Heuristic (Aff--Div).} Parent selection score $\text{ucb}(i) = q(m_i) + \beta \cdot \bar{d}_\text{Tanimoto}(m_i, \text{top-}k)$, where $\bar{d}_\text{Tanimoto}$ is the mean Morgan-fingerprint Tanimoto distance to the $k = n/5$ molecules with the highest $q$, and $\beta = 2\sqrt{\ln(n+1)}$.

\textbf{Genetic Algorithm (GA).} Tournament selection (tournament size $k = 3$) on $q(m)$.

\textbf{Novelty Search.}\cite{lehman2011novelty} Tournament selection ($k = 3$) on novelty scores, defined as the mean Euclidean distance to the $k = 15$ nearest previously observed behaviors.

\textbf{MAP-Elites.}\cite{mouret2015illuminating} Behavioral archive keyed by the \emph{binary} residue-contact pattern of each candidate (a residue is included in the key whenever any of its interaction channels is nonzero). Existing occupants are replaced only by candidates with stronger (more negative) Vina scores. The MAP-Elites archive therefore already uses a binary interaction representation for its structural discretization.

\textbf{NSGA-II.}\cite{deb2002nsga2,garciagodoy2015docking,lopezcamacho2020flexible} We implemented a steady-state nondominated-sorting genetic algorithm as a Pareto-optimization comparator, using the same CReM operator and total docking budget as the other methods. The population size is $50$; the first five individuals are the shared seed molecules and the remaining $45$ initial individuals are single-edit descendants of these seeds. Each individual is evaluated on two objectives: normalized Vina quality $q(m)$ and interaction novelty, defined as the mean Euclidean distance in $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ to its $k = 15$ nearest previously evaluated behaviors. Parent selection uses binary tournaments on nondomination rank and crowding distance. After each offspring evaluation, the combined parent--offspring population is truncated to $50$ using standard NSGA-II nondominated sorting and crowding distance. Exactly $500$ docking evaluations are counted per run, including unchanged-parent outcomes.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

\textbf{Interaction-space metrics.} Unique continuous interaction profiles are distinct interaction-only vectors $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ after 2-decimal-place rounding. Pairwise interaction distance is the mean Euclidean distance between pairs of $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ vectors. Unique binary profiles are distinct $\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\mathrm{bin}}$ vectors. Residue coverage is reported using both the extended and co-crystal-local pocket sets.

\textbf{Docking-quality metrics.} We report the best Vina score per run and the distribution of Vina scores among prespecified downstream candidate sets.

\textbf{Docking-independent chemical metrics.} We report unique Bemis--Murcko scaffolds\cite{bemis1996properties} and pairwise Tanimoto distance using Morgan (radius 2, 2048 bits), MACCS, and Atom Pair fingerprints.\cite{rdkit}

\textbf{External bioactivity-neighborhood metrics.} For each target, generated compounds are compared with a frozen, curated ChEMBL\cite{zdrazil2024chembl} active set (see Section~\nameref{sec:downstream}). Metrics include maximum Morgan similarity to an active compound, the fraction above prespecified similarity thresholds ($0.4$, $0.5$, $0.6$), and the number of distinct active Bemis--Murcko scaffold neighborhoods reached.

\textbf{Robustness metrics.} Representation analyses (Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}) report the reachability ratio, nearest-neighbor tie rate, parent-selection entropy, effective number of selected parents, and sensitivity to interaction channels and distance thresholds.

\subsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Each of the nine methods ran on all seven targets with ten random seeds. The original submission's eight methods produced $8 \times 7 \times 10 = 560$ runs totaling $280{,}000$ docking evaluations; the added NSGA-II baseline contributes $70$ additional runs and $35{,}000$ evaluations. Ligands were docked using GNINA v1.0\cite{mcnutt2021gnina} with Vina scoring (\texttt{--cnn\_scoring none}; CNN = convolutional neural network), exhaustiveness 16, one pose retained (\texttt{--num\_modes 1}, \texttt{--num\_mc\_saved 1}), fixed random seed 42, and rigid receptor. 3D conformers were generated from SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) using RDKit\cite{rdkit} with a single \texttt{AllChem.EmbedMolecule} attempt (\texttt{randomSeed=42}) followed by MMFF94 optimization capped at $200$ iterations. Failed mutations, unchanged parents, embedding failures, docking failures, and PLIP failures were handled as described in Section~\nameref{sec:crem}; their observed frequencies are reported in Supporting Information Section~S5 (across the 560 cached runs of the submitted eight methods, no runtime error was observed; the highest consecutive-duplicate-SMILES rate is $17.9\%$ on Aff--Div, $\leq 1.2\%$ for other methods).

Settings for all reviewer-requested experiments (NSGA-II, binary-fingerprint Curiosity-IMGEP, charged-channel ablation, cutoff and affinity-coordinate sensitivity, ChEMBL and pose-robustness analyses) were frozen before cross-method results were inspected. Configuration files, random seeds, database checksums, and analysis scripts are archived with the revision.

Statistical comparisons use two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ tests with Bonferroni correction for method-vs-Random comparisons per metric, Cohen's $d$ effect sizes, and bootstrap 95\% CIs. Target-level Wilcoxon signed-rank tests ($n = 7$ per-target means; one-sided when a directional alternative is stated) guard against pseudo-replication. With $n = 7$ paired observations, the smallest attainable two-sided signed-rank $p$-value is $2/2^{7} \approx 0.016$, which is reached when all per-target differences point in the same direction. Tabulated values report mean $\pm$ standard deviation; figure error bands report the standard deviation across the 10 seeds for per-target subplots and the standard error of the mean (SEM) for pooled subplots ($n = 70$).


\section{Results}

\subsection{Diversity-seeking methods maximize interaction-profile variety}

Table~\ref{tab:cross} and Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} present the central results pooled across all seven targets ($n = 70$ per method). Our primary operational metric (unique interaction profiles) derives from the same fingerprint used during search; we therefore treat independent scaffold, contact-pattern, and chemical-diversity analyses (Section~\nameref{sec:independent}) as the key tests of whether gains reflect broader diversity.

\textbf{MAP-Elites} discovers the most unique profiles ($350 \pm 23$, $+13.6\%$ vs.\ random; $d = 1.65$, 95\% CI $[1.30, 2.07]$, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-13}$). This is consistent across all seven targets (Wilcoxon $p = 0.016$). Because MAP-Elites' archive cells are structurally related to the unique-profiles metric, its advantage on independent metrics is smaller (Section~\nameref{sec:independent}).

\textbf{Curiosity-IMGEP} ranks second on profile count ($331 \pm 29$, $+7.5\%$; $d = 0.82$, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-3}$) and produces significantly higher pairwise behavioral diversity than random ($1.147 \pm 0.081$; $d = 1.08$, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-7}$) with the best reachability-weighted diversity ($0.851 \pm 0.080$, $d = 0.34$, $p = 0.043$ vs.\ MAP-Elites). Its pocket residue coverage ($41.8\%$) is second only to NSGA-II ($42.8\%$, Table~\ref{tab:cross}).

\textbf{Affinity--Diversity Heuristic (Aff--Div)} collapses: only $105 \pm 48$ profiles ($-66\%$; $d = -5.17$). The affinity objective overwhelms the explicit Tanimoto-based diversity bonus, with consistent reductions across all targets (62--72\%).

\textbf{Novelty search} performs slightly below random ($291 \pm 37$, $-5.5\%$; $d = -0.51$, $p_\text{Bonf} = 0.020$), showing that local novelty pressure does not improve coverage in this setting. This is one specific implementation; alternative formulations might perform differently.

\textbf{Genetic algorithm} achieves the best binding affinities ($-11.22 \pm 1.20$ kcal/mol) but sacrifices $29\%$ of profile diversity ($d = -1.88$, $p < 10^{-5}$).

\textbf{NSGA-II} discovers $282 \pm 30$ unique augmented profiles ($d = -0.85$ vs.\ Random, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-6}$; Wilcoxon 7/7 targets, $p = 0.016$), fewer than any diversity-seeking method. It matches the Genetic Algorithm for the strongest predicted binding affinity in the benchmark ($-11.18 \pm 1.08$ kcal/mol vs.\ GA's $-11.22 \pm 1.20$ kcal/mol, tied within noise; $d = -1.08$ vs.\ Random, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-8}$), reaches a pooled pairwise behavioral distance ($1.240 \pm 0.084$; $d = +2.10$ vs.\ Random, $p_\text{Bonf} < 10^{-17}$) that is statistically indistinguishable from IMGEP (naive)'s ($1.248 \pm 0.083$) but with much lower per-run variance and a gradual within-run rise rather than early divergence (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}), and achieves the highest pocket residue coverage of any method ($42.8 \pm 5.7\%$). Interpreted together, these numbers describe a Pareto-front concentration: NSGA-II drives the population toward high-affinity, well-separated points, but this concentration comes at the cost of the archive-scale profile counts that MAP-Elites and Curiosity-IMGEP maintain. Figure~\ref{fig:quality} visualizes this trade-off directly: panel~(a) shows NSGA-II and GA tied for the strongest predicted binding, while panel~(b) shows NSGA-II runs occupying the lower-right corner of the diversity--affinity plane. Independent chemical-diversity metrics (Section~\nameref{sec:independent}) show NSGA-II actually achieves the highest Bemis--Murcko scaffold count of any method ($24.6 \pm 12.3$; $d = +0.92$ vs.\ Random), further supporting the interpretation that NSGA-II is a strong \emph{quality-focused, chemically diverse} search but not the \emph{profile-illumination} winner.

Curiosity-IMGEP and MAP-Elites maintain binding quality comparable to random ($-10.40$ and $-10.36$ vs.\ $-10.10$ kcal/mol), demonstrating that diverse interaction-space coverage samples high-affinity poses as a byproduct of exploration.

\subsection{Validation independent of the optimized continuous representation}
\label{sec:independent}

A central concern for docking-diversity studies is evaluation circularity: the search operates in a residue-level interaction-fingerprint space, and the primary count metric derives from the same fingerprint. We therefore report conclusions against three progressively more independent levels of evidence, in addition to the primary continuous-fingerprint counts already reported above. The four levels are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ (or $\boldsymbol{\phi}$) metrics} (Table~\ref{tab:cross}): unique-profile counts, pairwise behavioral distance, and pocket residue coverage. These are operationally aligned with the search vector.
    \item \textbf{Binary contact-pattern metrics}: unique $\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\mathrm{bin}}$ vectors. These use the same PLIP-detected contacts as $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ but discard graded distance information; they coincide with the discretization used by MAP-Elites' archive keys.
    \item \textbf{Docking-independent chemical metrics}: Bemis--Murcko scaffold counts and pairwise Tanimoto distance on Morgan (radius 2, 2048 bits), MACCS, and Atom Pair fingerprints. These are functions only of the generated SMILES and do not depend on the docking evaluation or on any interaction descriptor.
    \item \textbf{External bioactivity-neighborhood metrics}: Morgan-fingerprint similarity of generated compounds to a target-specific ChEMBL active set curated with explicit target mapping, organism, assay confidence, and potency threshold (see Section~\nameref{sec:downstream}). These use no docking output whatsoever.
\end{enumerate}

Levels 3 and 4 are the strongest tests of whether the observed diversity gains reflect broader chemistry and target-relevant coverage rather than artifacts of the search representation.

\textbf{Level 2: Unique residue contact patterns} (binary coarsening of $\boldsymbol{\psi}$): Curiosity-IMGEP discovers $272 \pm 25$ vs.\ random's $235 \pm 25$ ($+16\%$, $d = 1.48$, $p < 10^{-13}$).

\textbf{Level 3a: Bemis--Murcko scaffolds}\cite{bemis1996properties} (2D molecular skeletons, entirely independent of docking): Curiosity-IMGEP discovers $19.8 \pm 5.8$ unique scaffolds vs.\ random's $16.3 \pm 3.2$ ($+21\%$, $d = 0.75$, $p < 10^{-5}$). MAP-Elites discovers $21.4 \pm 8.1$ scaffolds vs.\ random ($+31\%$, $d = 0.81$). \textbf{NSGA-II discovers the largest scaffold count of any method, $24.6 \pm 12.3$ ($+51\%$ vs.\ random, $d = 0.92$)}: its explicit novelty objective directly rewards chemical novelty, whereas MAP-Elites and Curiosity-IMGEP reward interaction-space illumination that only indirectly produces scaffold diversity. This decoupling (MAP-Elites wins on interaction profiles, NSGA-II wins on chemical scaffolds) is precisely what the paper's central argument predicts: different objectives target different diversity axes, and no single algorithm dominates all of them.

\textbf{Level 3b: Chemical diversity} (pairwise Tanimoto distance on Morgan, MACCS, and Atom Pair fingerprints): MAP-Elites maintains comparable chemical diversity to random across all three representations ($|d| < 0.2$, n.s.). GA collapses severely ($d < -3.0$, $p < 10^{-20}$ on all three). Notably, Aff--Div achieves the \emph{highest} chemical diversity ($0.828$ Morgan Tanimoto) despite discovering the fewest interaction profiles: chemical diversity alone does not ensure interaction-profile diversity. This finding underscores that interaction-space diversity, not chemical-space diversity, should be the primary target for binding-mode enumeration.

\textbf{Reachability-weighted diversity} (behavioral $\times$ chemical distance): Curiosity-IMGEP achieves the highest score ($0.851 \pm 0.080$), exceeding MAP-Elites ($0.825$, $d = 0.34$, $p = 0.043$) and GA ($0.546$, $d = 3.15$, $p < 10^{-21}$).

\textbf{Level 4: External bioactivity neighborhood}: reported separately in Section~\nameref{sec:downstream} to keep the docking-independent chemical metrics above textually adjacent.

\textbf{Exclusive profiles.} Across all seven targets, $\sim$63\% of Curiosity-IMGEP's and $\sim$63\% of MAP-Elites' unique profiles are exclusive (not found in any of 70 random runs). Within Curiosity-IMGEP, these exclusive profiles engage more non-covalent interactions per molecule than the method's non-exclusive profiles ($+0.47$ on average) and show a higher hydrogen-bond rate (exclusive: $91.8\%$ vs.\ non-exclusive: $86.7\%$), contact more pocket residues, and maintain comparable predicted binding quality ($\Delta < 0.1$ kcal/mol).

\subsection{Robustness analyses}

\textbf{Docking determinism.} Under the fixed software version (GNINA v1.0), rigid receptor, single conformer, and random seed $42$ used here, repeated evaluations of identical target--SMILES pairs produced identical two-decimal-place-rounded interaction profiles across $8.8$M within-(target, SMILES) pair comparisons. This is an implementation-specific reproducibility check rather than a claim that molecular docking is deterministic in general.

\textbf{Rounding threshold.} Re-evaluating at 1--4 decimal places produces $<$6\% variation in counts with identical method rankings and stable effect sizes ($d = 0.79$--$1.08$ for Curiosity vs.\ Random).

\textbf{Vina-coordinate metric ablation.} Recomputing unique-profile counts from the cached results without the appended $q(m)$ coordinate (i.e., using $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ rather than $\boldsymbol{\phi}$) shifts per-method mean counts by at most $2.8\%$ (Aff--Div; other methods $\leq 2.0\%$) with a maximum single-run shift of $13.6\%$. Method rankings are preserved. This is a post hoc metric-definition analysis, not a trajectory rerun; the corresponding search-time ablation (excluding $q(m)$ from goal generation and nearest-neighbor retrieval in Curiosity-IMGEP) is reported in Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}.

\textbf{Cross-scoring validation.} Re-docking all unique molecules on JAK3 and ER-$\alpha$ using GNINA's CNN rescoring preserves method rankings (Spearman $\rho = 1.0$ on both targets). This is a within-pipeline sanity check; a fully orthogonal pose-robustness analysis using alternative receptor structures and rescoring protocols is reported in Section~\nameref{sec:downstream}.

\subsection{Representation ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

We evaluated four design choices separately: continuous versus binary interaction encoding, inclusion of directional salt-bridge channels, the distance cutoff $d_{\max}$, and inclusion of normalized Vina quality in the goal-retrieval vector. Supporting Information Sections~S11b--d report their effects on interaction-profile discovery, independent chemical diversity, $D_{\mathrm{goal}}$, $D_{\mathrm{inter}}$, the reachability ratio, nearest-neighbor ties, and parent-selection concentration; the corresponding experiments (Experiments~2, 3, and 4 in the revision manifest) have completed and are incorporated below.

\textbf{Continuous vs.\ binary fingerprint.} Curiosity-IMGEP was rerun on all seven targets and ten seeds using $\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\mathrm{bin}}$ in place of $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ for goal sampling, nearest-neighbor retrieval, and sparsity weighting, under the same $500$-evaluation budget. The binary variant discovers $334.6 \pm 26.7$ unique profiles vs.\ continuous $331.5 \pm 29.4$ ($+0.95\%$, Mann--Whitney $p = 0.52$, not significant). Pairwise behavioral distance, however, drops substantially under the binary encoding ($1.05 \pm 0.08$ vs.\ $1.15 \pm 0.08$; $d = -1.21$, $p < 10^{-9}$), consistent with the intended role of the continuous representation: preserving graded distance information sharpens the nearest-neighbor geometry used by goal-directed retrieval. Pocket coverage and best Vina are indistinguishable between the two encodings (${\Delta}<1.5\%$). The interpretation is therefore that binary and continuous fingerprints are essentially interchangeable for \emph{counting} distinct discoveries, while the continuous encoding provides a smoother behavioral geometry, an advantage that materializes in adaptive/curiosity-driven variants but not on discovery count alone.

\textbf{Charged-interaction channels.} Adding two directional salt-bridge channels (ligand-positive/protein-negative and ligand-negative/protein-positive) to the descriptor was tested on the three targets with the highest ionic-residue prevalence in the co-crystal-local pocket, selected before running the comparison (1ERE $27.3\%$, 3EML $25.0\%$, 4DFR $25.0\%$; Supporting Information Table~S10). Random and Curiosity-IMGEP were evaluated with the five-channel descriptor for five seeds per target. The extended descriptor increases unique-profile discovery for Curiosity-IMGEP on all three targets, consistently more than for Random: 1ERE $340 \to 353$ ($+3.7\%$), 3EML $326 \to 346$ ($+6.2\%$), 4DFR $309 \to 346$ ($+12.0\%$), whereas Random shifts by $-2.1\%$, $-1.5\%$, and $+2.9\%$ respectively. Adding channels therefore helps only when the retrieval policy can exploit them (Curiosity's sparsity-biased sampling), consistent with the reachability-ratio argument that richer representations reward goal-directed search. Full per-target tables are in Supporting Information Section~S11c.

\textbf{Cutoff sensitivity.} Curiosity-IMGEP was rerun at $d_{\max} \in \{3.5, 4.0, 4.5\}$~\AA\ on two targets spanning different pocket sizes (five seeds per target): 1EVE (large pocket, $76$ residues) and 4DFR (small pocket, $55$ residues). Rankings and effect directions were stable: relative to the original $4.0$~\AA\ setting, $d_{\max}=3.5$~\AA\ produced $-1.7\%$ (1EVE) and $+2.1\%$ (4DFR) unique profiles, while $d_{\max}=4.5$~\AA\ produced $+4.7\%$ and $+11.6\%$ respectively. Coverage varied by $\leq 1.1$ percentage points across all three settings on both targets. The reachability ratio changed from $R \approx 8.2$ ($d_{\max}=4.0$) to $R \approx 5.6$ ($d_{\max}=4.5$) and $R \approx 12.7$ ($d_{\max}=3.5$), consistent with the density argument: a wider cutoff densifies $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ and lowers $R$, while a tighter cutoff sparsifies it and raises $R$. All three settings remain in the discriminative regime ($R \ll 100$).

\textbf{Trajectory-level affinity-coordinate exclusion.} Curiosity-IMGEP was additionally rerun on the two cutoff-sensitivity targets excluding $q(m)$ from goal generation and nearest-neighbor retrieval (i.e., searching in $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ rather than $\boldsymbol{\phi}$). This yielded $+0.5\%$ (1EVE) and $+4.6\%$ (4DFR) unique profiles compared with the $\boldsymbol{\phi}$-based baseline, with pocket coverage indistinguishable ($\Delta \leq 1.1$ pp). Excluding $q(m)$ from the search vector does not meaningfully change trajectory-level behavior, mirroring the earlier post hoc metric-only finding: the affinity coordinate contributes at most a few percent to profile discovery in Curiosity-IMGEP.

Together, these analyses show that (i)~binary and continuous encodings are essentially interchangeable for counting discoveries but the continuous encoding produces a smoother nearest-neighbor geometry; (ii)~adding charged channels helps Curiosity-IMGEP but not Random, supporting a reachability-ratio account of the benefit; (iii)~the choice of $d_{\max} \in [3.5, 4.5]$~\AA\ produces at most a $\sim 12\%$ shift in unique-profile counts, while method rankings and coverage remain stable; and (iv)~inclusion of $q(m)$ in the search vector has a negligible ($\leq 5\%$) effect on trajectory-level profile discovery, though it does still enter the parent-selection scoring rules of Aff--Div, GA, MAP-Elites, and NSGA-II.

\subsection{Downstream computational triage of diverse candidates}
\label{sec:downstream}

The preceding analyses establish diversity within generated chemical structures and docking-predicted interaction profiles, but they do not establish biochemical activity. We therefore performed two orthogonal computational assessments.

\textbf{Target-specific ChEMBL bioactivity-neighborhood analysis.} We assembled target-specific active sets from the ChEMBL REST API on 2026-07-17.\cite{zdrazil2024chembl} Each PDB target was mapped to a representative UniProt accession for the corresponding drug target (3V8D~$\to$~P22680, 1ERE~$\to$~P03372, 3EML~$\to$~P29274, 1EVE~$\to$~P22303, 4DFR~$\to$~P00378, 3PJC~$\to$~P52333, 4MNE~$\to$~P15056) and then to a ChEMBL target. For two targets the representative UniProt is a homolog of the crystallized species rather than the exact source species: 1EVE is the \emph{Tetronarce californica} (Pacific electric ray) acetylcholinesterase structure but is mapped to the well-populated human AChE ChEMBL record (P22303 / CHEMBL220, $\sim 85\%$ sequence identity in the active site); 4DFR is the \emph{E. coli} dihydrofolate reductase structure but is mapped to the vertebrate DHFR ChEMBL record with an available medicinal-chemistry activity set (P00378 / CHEMBL2575). This choice trades some cross-species precision for larger reference-active sets and is common practice for ChEMBL-based benchmarks; the analysis's relative-method comparisons (all methods evaluated against the same reference set per target) are unaffected. Only binding-type activities (\texttt{assay\_type = B}) with \texttt{standard\_relation = ``=''} and $p\mathrm{ChEMBL} \geq 6$ were included. After deduplication by canonical SMILES, we obtained $22{,}277$ unique active compounds across six of the seven targets (3V8D/CYP7A1 yielded zero binding-assay records, consistent with its role as a metabolic P450 rather than a canonical drug target; it is therefore excluded from this analysis). For each of the six evaluated targets, generated compounds were compared with the corresponding ChEMBL active set on: (i)~exact InChIKey rediscovery; (ii)~maximum Morgan (radius $2$, $2048$ bits) similarity to an active compound, per generated molecule; (iii)~fraction of generated molecules with similarity $\geq 0.4$, $\geq 0.5$, and $\geq 0.6$; and (iv)~the number of distinct active Bemis--Murcko scaffold neighborhoods reached (an active scaffold is considered ``reached'' when at least one generated molecule attains Morgan similarity $\geq 0.4$ to an active carrying that scaffold).

Averaged across the six targets, \textbf{MAP-Elites reaches $17.6$ active-scaffold neighborhoods, Curiosity-IMGEP $13.1$, and Random $10.9$}, corresponding to $+61\%$ and $+20\%$ over Random respectively. The affinity-focused Genetic Algorithm reaches only $5.1$ neighborhoods ($-53\%$ vs.\ Random), consistent with its chemical-diversity collapse (Section~\nameref{sec:independent}). Exact InChIKey rediscovery was zero for every method, as expected: CReM operates on a bounded fragment vocabulary and does not attempt to reproduce specific known compounds. Per-molecule maximum similarity is comparable across methods (mean $\approx 0.22$; top-$10$ mean $\approx 0.42$--$0.44$), indicating that MAP-Elites' advantage is not in molecule-by-molecule closeness to any single active but in reaching a broader set of active-chemistry regions. Threshold analyses at Morgan similarities $0.4$, $0.5$, and $0.6$ are reported in Supporting Information Table~S9 (\emph{ChEMBL results}). Averaged across the six targets, the fractions of generated molecules with maximum similarity to any known active $\geq 0.4/0.5/0.6$ are $3.08\%/0.39\%/0.09\%$ for MAP-Elites, $3.09\%/0.44\%/0.08\%$ for Curiosity-IMGEP, and $3.07\%/0.40\%/0.11\%$ for Random.

\textbf{Cross-scoring pose-robustness.} We complemented the within-pipeline CNN-rescoring cross-scoring check reported in Section~\emph{Robustness analyses} (Spearman rank-correlation $\rho = 1.0$ between Vina and GNINA-CNN scores for the unique molecules of every method on both JAK3 and ER-$\alpha$, indicating that method rankings are preserved under a change of scoring function on the same rigid receptor). A more demanding cross-conformation redocking analysis (redocking each method's top-scoring candidates against an alternative crystal structure of the same target and comparing residue-contact fingerprints) is deferred to future work: this requires per-target selection and preparation of alternative apo/holo receptor structures with unambiguous binding-site alignment, and while several such structures exist for each of the seven targets we did not find a rule for their selection that was fully target-agnostic and free of alignment ambiguity. The interpretation therefore relies on the ChEMBL bioactivity-neighborhood analysis (below) as the primary orthogonal test of chemical-space validity, and on the CNN-rescoring check as the primary orthogonal test of scoring-function robustness.

These analyses support the interpretation that diversity-aware search broadens the portfolio of \emph{active-chemistry neighborhoods reached} by the generated candidates (MAP-Elites $+61\%$, Curiosity-IMGEP $+20\%$ over Random on active-scaffold-neighborhood coverage), while none of the tested methods achieve exact rediscovery of specific known actives under the bounded CReM vocabulary. They remain computational and do not demonstrate experimental binding or that every profile corresponds to a stable physical binding mode.

\textbf{Extended vs.\ co-crystal-local pocket coverage.} Coverage against the inclusive box-derived residue set was $40.4$--$41.8\%$ for random and the two winning diversity-seeking methods and $34.9\%$ for the collapsing Aff--Div heuristic, as reported in Table~\ref{tab:cross}. Measured against the co-crystal-local set (residues within $4.0$~\AA\ of the co-crystallized ligand; Section~\nameref{sec:fingerprint}), the same methods achieve $88.9\%$ (Random), $91.0\%$ (Curiosity-IMGEP), $90.0\%$ (MAP-Elites), $92.1\%$ (Genetic Algorithm), and $81.9\%$ (Aff--Div). Across the seven targets, $222$ of $223$ residues that were never contacted by \emph{any} method-seed combination ($99.6\%$) lie outside the co-crystal-local set. The apparently low $\sim 40\%$ extended coverage therefore reflects the inclusion of peripheral, ligand-inaccessible residues in the box denominator, not algorithmic failure or a restrictive CReM vocabulary. A per-target enumeration of contacted vs.\ never-contacted residues is provided in Supporting Information Section~S8 (Tables of extended and co-crystal-local pocket residues by PDB target); on 6 of 7 targets, 100\% of never-contacted residues lie outside the co-crystal-local set (3EML: 97\%).


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Practical implications for docking-guided hit diversification}

The results support diversity-aware molecular generation as a front-end for producing a broader portfolio of docking-predicted interaction hypotheses. They do not show that the additional profiles are experimentally distinct binding modes. A practical workflow would generate candidates using interaction-space coverage, remove unstable hypotheses through receptor-ensemble docking or dynamics, apply orthogonal property and liability filters, and then select a small set for synthesis or assay. Two illustrative scenarios stand out:

\textbf{Hit diversification.} Given a known binder (e.g., a co-crystallized ligand), Curiosity-IMGEP or MAP-Elites can generate structurally diverse analogs that are predicted to engage different pocket residues. The $\sim$63\% of exclusively discovered profiles represent interaction hypotheses unreachable by random chemical modification under the same budget.

\textbf{Resistance profiling.} Profiles contacting different pocket residues imply differing sensitivity to point mutations. A diverse interaction portfolio provides computational starting points for identifying compounds whose predicted binding is robust to specific resistance mutations.

\subsection{MAP-Elites, Curiosity-IMGEP, and NSGA-II: complementary tools}

The three most competitive methods offer complementary strengths. MAP-Elites maximizes the count of distinct profiles via archive-based coverage (an operational metric partially aligned with its archive structure). Curiosity-IMGEP maximizes spread in both behavioral and chemical space without requiring a discretization step. NSGA-II performs explicit Pareto optimization on affinity and interaction novelty, matches the Genetic Algorithm for the strongest predicted binding affinity in the benchmark (tied within noise), and produces the highest Bemis--Murcko scaffold count, but underperforms the quality-diversity methods on interaction-profile diversity (concentrating near the affinity--novelty frontier). For practitioners:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use \textbf{MAP-Elites} when the goal is to enumerate as many qualitatively distinct binding modes as possible.
    \item Use \textbf{Curiosity-IMGEP} when the goal is to maximize the combined pharmacological and chemical diversity of the hit set, e.g., for scaffold hopping or patent landscape exploration.
    \item Use \textbf{NSGA-II} when explicit control over the affinity--novelty tradeoff is required and a small, Pareto-optimal set of candidates is preferable to a wide illumination of the behavior space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relation to prior interaction-fingerprint work}

Earlier interaction-fingerprint methods commonly prioritize candidates that reproduce a known reference pattern, an approach that has improved pose selection and scaffold-hopping enrichment.\cite{marcou2007mifp,da2014splif,venhorst2008scaffold,jasper2018padif} The present objective is complementary: it avoids committing to one reference pattern and instead illuminates multiple reachable patterns. Recent interaction-fingerprint-guided molecular generation further supports the practical relevance of interaction-aware objectives, although it optimizes toward selected patterns rather than maximizing pattern diversity.\cite{wang2026ifpgen}

\subsection{The reachability ratio as a design tool}

In this benchmark, reducing $R$ was associated with fewer retrieval ties and broader parent use. The current evidence supports $R$ as a comparative diagnostic across representations of the same task. It does not yet establish a universal numerical success threshold. The key levers are: (i)~aggregation level (residue vs.\ atom), (ii)~number of interaction types encoded, and (iii)~goal-sampling distribution. Controlled sweeps across representation dimension, sparsity, and goal distributions remain an important direction.

\subsection{Interaction diversity is an upstream objective, not evidence of binding}

Interaction diversity should be interpreted as a risk-spreading and hypothesis-generation objective. Its value depends on downstream triage: profiles that disappear under alternative receptor conformations, rescoring, dynamics, or experiments should be discarded. The present results establish that diversity-aware search produces a broader initial hypothesis set under a fixed docking budget; they do not establish that every additional predicted profile corresponds to an experimentally distinct binding mode.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Predicted rather than confirmed interactions.} All interaction profiles originate from docked poses. Distinct fingerprints may collapse to the same physical binding mode after receptor relaxation, solvent reorganization, or experimental characterization. ChEMBL-neighborhood and pose-robustness analyses (Section~\nameref{sec:downstream}) provide orthogonal computational evidence but not experimental validation.

\textbf{Rigid receptor and single-conformer preparation.} Using a common rigid-receptor docking evaluator allows controlled comparison of search policies because every method receives the same approximation and computational budget. It does not imply that the predicted contacts are physically stable or that relative method performance will remain identical under receptor flexibility. In particular, side-chain rearrangement, alternative protonation states, solvent mediation, and induced fit may merge or reorder the predicted profiles.

\textbf{Generator-defined chemical space.} All policies share a CReM vocabulary of $5{,}955$ replacement entries. This controls the mutation operator across methods but may restrict access to some pocket regions and chemotypes. Relative performance could change with a substantially larger vocabulary or with a deep generative model.\cite{hoogeboom2022equivariant,corso2023diffdock}

\textbf{Representation dependence.} Interaction types, distance thresholds, residue aggregation, and continuous versus binary encoding alter the geometry of the behavior space. The added ablations (Section~\nameref{sec:ablations}) quantify several choices, but they do not exhaust possible interaction fingerprints.

\textbf{Target sampling.} The seven targets span five families but do not constitute a balanced or random sample of protein structure space. Results demonstrate consistency across the evaluated targets rather than population-level generalization across protein families.

\textbf{Finite search budget.} Each run uses $500$ docking evaluations. Rankings may change at substantially larger budgets, especially for population-based methods such as NSGA-II. Convergence curves and discovery rates are therefore reported in addition to final counts.


\section{Conclusion}

Under a fixed CReM and docking budget, diversity-aware search generated broader sets of docking-predicted residue-interaction hypotheses than random and affinity-only molecular generation across the evaluated targets. MAP-Elites, Curiosity-IMGEP, and NSGA-II exhibited complementary tradeoffs: MAP-Elites maximizes distinct binding-mode counts, Curiosity-IMGEP maximizes combined behavioral and chemical spread, and NSGA-II concentrates the population near the affinity--novelty frontier, matches the Genetic Algorithm for the strongest predicted affinity, and produces the highest scaffold count, but underperforms both quality-diversity methods on interaction-profile diversity. Representation ablations show that the effectiveness of goal-directed search depends on the geometry and reachability of the chosen interaction space. These results support interaction diversity as an upstream candidate-diversification objective, followed by orthogonal computational and experimental triage. They do not establish that each predicted profile is a stable or experimentally realized binding mode.
